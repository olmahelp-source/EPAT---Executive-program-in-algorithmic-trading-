## 36. Descriptive statistics of returns (skewness, kurtosis, fat tails)

**Formulas**
```
mean        m  = (1/n) * sum(x_i)
sample var  s2 = sum((x_i - m)^2) / (n - 1)
skewness       = E[(x - m)^3] / sigma^3
excess kurt.   = E[(x - m)^4] / sigma^4 - 3          (normal distribution = 0)
```

**Worked example.** Ten daily returns: `1.2%, -0.8%, 0.4%, 1.5%, -2.1%, 0.9%, 0.2%, -0.5%, 1.8%, -1.2%`.
- mean = 0.14%, sample std = 1.269%
- skewness = -0.34 (a slightly longer left tail)
- excess kurtosis = -1.00 (looks light-tailed, but 10 points say almost nothing about tails)

Now simulate 200,000 returns from a Student-t distribution with 5 degrees of freedom, scaled to 1% daily std. Theory says excess kurtosis = 6 / (nu - 4) = 6. The sample gives **4.15**. Sample kurtosis is unstable because it depends on the most extreme observations.

**Code:** `t36_descriptive`
**Watch out:** never judge tail risk from a small sample, and report median and MAD next to mean and std.

## 37. Probability distributions (normal, Student-t, log-normal, stable, mixtures)

**Formulas**
```
Normal:     f(x) = exp(-(x-mu)^2 / (2 s^2)) / (s * sqrt(2 pi))
Student-t:  f(x) = G((v+1)/2) / (G(v/2) * sqrt(v pi) * s) * (1 + ((x-mu)/s)^2 / v)^(-(v+1)/2)
Log-normal: if ln(P_T/P_0) ~ Normal then P_T/P_0 is log-normal
AIC = 2k - 2 ln(L)        (smaller is better)
```

**Worked example.** 2,000 simulated returns from t(5), 1% std.

| Model | Parameters | Log-likelihood | AIC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 2 | 6350.9 | -12,697.8 |
| Student-t (best df = 5) | 3 | 6444.8 | -12,883.6 |

The t model wins by about 186 AIC points, a decisive difference. The grid search over degrees of freedom picks df = 5, the true value. (The t scale is moment-matched, so this is an approximation to full maximum likelihood.)

**Code:** `t37_distributions`
**Watch out:** a normal fit can look fine in the middle of the distribution and still be wrong where risk lives, in the tails.

## 38. Hypothesis testing and p-values in strategy validation

**Formulas**
```
H0: mean return = 0
t = mean / (s / sqrt(n))
p (two-sided) = 2 * (1 - Phi(|t|))
annualized Sharpe = mean/s * sqrt(252)
```

**Worked example.** One year (n = 250) of daily returns with mean 0.05% and std 1%.
- t = 0.0005 / (0.01 / sqrt(250)) = **0.79**
- p = **0.43**
- annualized Sharpe = 0.79

A Sharpe near 0.8 sounds respectable, yet one year of data cannot tell it from luck. At 5% significance you need |t| above about 2, so a Sharpe of about 2 over one year.

**Code:** `t38_ttest`
**Watch out:** a p-value is the probability of data this extreme if the strategy had no edge. It is not the probability that the strategy works.

## 39. Multiple-testing correction (Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg)

**Formulas**
```
Bonferroni:  reject if p < alpha / m
Benjamini-Hochberg (control false discovery rate q):
   sort p(1) <= ... <= p(m); find the largest k with p(k) <= (k/m) * q; reject the first k
```

**Worked example.** Ten strategy variants with p-values `0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.03, 0.04, 0.2, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9`.

| Method | Threshold | Rejections |
|---|---|---|
| Naive (p < 0.05) | 0.05 | 5 |
| Bonferroni | 0.05 / 10 = 0.005 | 1 |
| Benjamini-Hochberg (q = 0.05) | 0.005, 0.010, 0.015, 0.020, 0.025 for ranks 1 to 5 | 3 |

Rank 3 passes (0.012 <= 0.015). Rank 4 fails (0.030 > 0.020). So BH keeps three.

**Code:** `t39_multiple_testing`
**Watch out:** the count m must include every variant you tried, not only the ones you reported.

## 40. Deflated Sharpe ratio and probability of backtest overfitting

**Formulas** (all Sharpe values per period, kurtosis not excess)
```
PSR(SR*) = Phi( (SR - SR*) * sqrt(n - 1) / sqrt(1 - skew*SR + (kurt - 1)/4 * SR^2) )
E[max SR] ~ sqrt(V) * [ (1 - g) * Phi^-1(1 - 1/N) + g * Phi^-1(1 - 1/(N e)) ]     g = 0.5772
DSR = PSR evaluated at SR* = E[max SR]
```
V is the variance of Sharpe ratios across the N trials.

**Worked example.** Best backtest: annual Sharpe 1.5 over 3 years (n = 756 days), skew -0.5, kurtosis 5. You tried N = 100 variants whose annual Sharpe dispersion is 0.5.
- daily SR = 1.5 / sqrt(252) = 0.0945
- expected maximum daily SR among 100 unskilled trials = 0.0797, which is **1.27 annualized**
- PSR against zero = 0.994 (looks great)
- **Deflated Sharpe = 0.654**, well below the usual 0.95 bar

The best of 100 tries is expected to show an annual Sharpe of 1.27 by luck alone. Your 1.5 barely beats that.

**Code:** `t40_deflated_sharpe` (and `psr`, `expected_max_sr` in `common.py`)
**Watch out:** use the honest trial count. Hiding failed variants is the main way this metric gets gamed.

## 41. Bootstrap, jackknife, and resampling

**Method (moving-block bootstrap)**
```
1. Cut the return series into overlapping blocks of length L.
2. Draw blocks with replacement, glue them until you have n observations.
3. Compute the statistic. Repeat B times. The spread of the B values is the standard error.
```

**Worked example.** 1,000 returns with autocorrelation 0.3 (daily Sharpe 0.1076). Bootstrap standard error of the Sharpe:
- i.i.d. bootstrap (L = 1): **0.0333**
- block bootstrap (L = 20): **0.0415**, about 25% wider

The i.i.d. version ignores serial dependence and reports false precision.

**Code:** `t41_bootstrap` (and `block_indices` in `common.py`)
**Watch out:** choose L at least as long as the dependence you want to preserve.

## 42. Bayesian inference and belief updating

**Formulas** (normal data, known sigma, normal prior)
```
posterior precision = n/sigma^2 + 1/tau^2
posterior mean      = (n/sigma^2 * xbar + mu0/tau^2) / posterior precision
```

**Worked example.** 60 days of a new strategy: xbar = 0.10% per day, sigma = 1%. Skeptical prior: mean 0, sd 0.05% (tau).
- precision = 60 / 0.0001 + 1 / 0.00000025 = 600,000 + 4,000,000 = 4,600,000
- posterior mean = **0.0130%** per day, posterior sd = 0.0466%
- weight on the data = 13%, weight on the prior = 87%

Sixty days is too little evidence to move a skeptical prior far. The raw 0.10% per day shrinks to 0.013%.

**Code:** `t42_bayes`
**Watch out:** state the prior before you look at the data, and test how sensitive the result is to it.

## 43. Maximum likelihood and method-of-moments estimation

**Formulas** (normal data)
```
MLE:  mu_hat = xbar,    sigma2_hat = (1/n) * sum((x - xbar)^2)      (biased low by (n-1)/n)
unbiased:               s2         = (1/(n-1)) * sum((x - xbar)^2)
```

**Worked example.** Data `2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9`: mean = 5. MLE variance = 32/8 = **4.0**. Unbiased variance = 32/7 = **4.571**. The ratio is 7/8, exactly (n-1)/n.

**Code:** `t43_mle`
**Watch out:** MLE is not automatically unbiased, and for fat-tailed models the likelihood can be flat and slow to converge.

## 44. Confidence intervals for performance metrics

**Formulas** (per-period Sharpe SR, n observations)
```
SE(SR), i.i.d. normal:      sqrt( (1 + 0.5*SR^2) / n )
SE(SR), skew and kurtosis:  sqrt( (1 - skew*SR + (kurt-1)/4 * SR^2) / n )
annual SE = SE(SR) * sqrt(252)
CI 95% = SR +/- 1.96 * SE
```

**Worked example.** Annual Sharpe 1.0 over 5 years (n = 1,260 days).
- SE (i.i.d.) = **0.448** annualized
- 95% CI = **[0.12, 1.88]**
- with skew -0.5, kurtosis 5: SE = 0.455 (slightly wider)

Five years of a Sharpe-1 strategy only tells you the true Sharpe is somewhere between "barely positive" and "excellent".

**Code:** `t44_sharpe_ci`
**Watch out:** always publish the interval next to the point estimate.

## 45. Autocorrelation, partial autocorrelation, Ljung-Box test

**Formulas**
```
rho_k = sum_{t>k}( (x_t - m)(x_{t-k} - m) ) / sum_t (x_t - m)^2
Ljung-Box  Q(h) = n (n+2) * sum_{k=1..h} rho_k^2 / (n - k)     ~ chi-square(h) under no autocorrelation
```
Critical value chi-square(5), 5% = 11.07.

**Worked example.** 500 values from an AR(1) series with phi = 0.3.
- sample rho_1..rho_5 = `0.277, 0.160, 0.080, -0.014, 0.045`
- Q(5) = **55.7**, far above 11.07, so white noise is rejected

**Code:** `t45_autocorr`
**Watch out:** apparent autocorrelation in prices can come from stale quotes and bid-ask bounce, not from tradable predictability.

## 46. Stationarity tests (ADF, KPSS, Phillips-Perron)

**Formula** (Dickey-Fuller regression with constant)
```
dy_t = a + gamma * y_{t-1} + e_t        H0: gamma = 0 (unit root)
test statistic = gamma_hat / SE(gamma_hat)        5% critical value = -2.86
```

**Worked example.** 400 simulations of 500 observations each.

| Series | Fraction rejecting the unit root at 5% |
|---|---|
| Random walk (a unit root exists) | **5.5%** (the false-rejection rate matches 5%) |
| AR(1) with phi = 0.5 (stationary) | **100%** |

**Code:** `t46_stationarity`
**Watch out:** pair ADF (null: non-stationary) with KPSS (null: stationary). Agreement between the two is more convincing than either alone.

## 47. Cointegration (Engle-Granger, Johansen)

**Method (Engle-Granger)**
```
1. Regress y_t = a + b * x_t + u_t          (both non-stationary)
2. Run the Dickey-Fuller test on the residuals u_t
   Use the Engle-Granger critical value (about -3.34 at 5% for two series), not -2.86.
```

**Worked example.** 300 simulations of 500 observations.
- y = 2x + AR(1) noise (truly cointegrated): estimated b = **2.0002**, residual test rejects in **100%** of runs
- **Independent** random walks: the naive regression t-statistic exceeds 2 in **86%** of runs, average R-squared = **0.22**, yet the Engle-Granger test rejects only **3.7%** of the time

That is the spurious-regression trap: two unrelated random walks look related in an ordinary regression.

**Code:** `t47_cointegration`
**Watch out:** a pair that was cointegrated in-sample can stop being so. Retest in rolling windows.

## 48. Regression (OLS, ridge, lasso, elastic net, robust)

**Formulas**
```
OLS:    b = (X'X)^-1 X'y
Ridge:  b = (X'X + lambda I)^-1 X'y
Lasso:  minimize ||y - Xb||^2 + lambda * sum|b_j|        (sets some b_j exactly to 0)
```

**Worked example.** Two features with correlation 0.998, true model y = 1*x1 + 0*x2 + noise. Fit on each half of 120 points:

| Fit | First half | Second half |
|---|---|---|
| OLS | (1.95, -0.97) | (2.06, -1.02) |
| Ridge, lambda = 10 | (0.47, 0.44) | (0.50, 0.46) |

OLS coefficients are large with opposite signs and flip depending on the sample. Ridge splits the effect evenly and stays stable.

**Code:** `t48_regression`
**Watch out:** with highly correlated features, individual coefficients are not interpretable even when predictions are fine.

## 49. Heteroscedasticity and Newey-West correction

**Formula** (standard error of a mean, lag window L)
```
omega = g0 + 2 * sum_{k=1..L} (1 - k/(L+1)) * g_k        g_k = autocovariance at lag k
SE_NW = sqrt(omega / n)
common choice: L = floor(4 * (n/100)^(2/9))
```

**Worked example.** 2,000 observations of an AR(1) with phi = 0.5, L = 7.
- naive SE = **0.0254**
- Newey-West SE = **0.0393** (1.55 times larger)
- infinite-lag theory: sqrt((1+phi)/(1-phi)) = 1.73

Using the naive SE would overstate the t-statistic by about 55%.

**Code:** `t49_newey_west`
**Watch out:** the correction changes standard errors, not coefficients. It does not fix a misspecified model.

## 50. Time-series models (AR, MA, ARIMA, SARIMA, VAR, VECM)

**Formulas**
```
AR(p):   x_t = c + phi_1 x_{t-1} + ... + phi_p x_{t-p} + e_t
AIC      = n * ln(RSS/n) + 2k
```

**Worked example.** Data from AR(2) with phi = (0.5, -0.3). Fit p = 1..5 on the first 1,000 points.

| p | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AIC | 67.1 | **-5.4** | -3.0 | -2.3 | -0.1 |

AIC picks p = 2, with fitted coefficients (0.495, -0.271). On the next 500 points the model's one-step mean squared error is **1.07** against **1.47** for forecasting zero.

**Code:** `t50_ar_selection`
**Watch out:** in real return series the improvement over the naive forecast is usually tiny. Always compare against a naive baseline out of sample.

## 51. Volatility models (EWMA, GARCH, EGARCH, GJR, realized vol, stochastic vol)

**Formulas**
```
EWMA:   s2_t = lambda * s2_{t-1} + (1 - lambda) * r_{t-1}^2                 (lambda = 0.94 typical)
GARCH:  s2_t = omega + alpha * r_{t-1}^2 + beta * s2_{t-1}
long-run variance V = omega / (1 - alpha - beta)
h-step forecast: s2_{t+h} = V + (alpha + beta)^(h-1) * (s2_{t+1} - V)
```

**Worked example.** Yesterday's variance 0.0001 (1.00% vol), today's return -2%.
- EWMA: 0.94 x 0.0001 + 0.06 x 0.0004 = 0.000118, so vol = **1.086%**
- GARCH with omega = 2e-6, alpha = 0.08, beta = 0.90: long-run vol = **1.00%**, next-day variance = 0.000124 (vol 1.114%)

| Horizon (days) | 1 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GARCH vol forecast | 1.114% | 1.105% | 1.095% | 1.079% |

Volatility decays slowly back toward the 1% long-run level.

**Code:** `t51_vol_models`
**Watch out:** judge volatility forecasts out of sample with a loss such as QLIKE, not by in-sample fit.

## 52. Regime detection (HMM, Markov switching, change-point detection)

**Method (Hamilton filter, two states)**
```
predicted_t  = filtered_{t-1} * P                      P = transition matrix
filtered_t  ~ predicted_t * density(x_t | state)       then normalize to sum to 1
```
This uses only past data. Smoothed probabilities use future data and leak in a backtest.

**Worked example.** Low-vol state (mean 0.05%, vol 0.7%) and high-vol state (mean -0.10%, vol 2.0%), stay probabilities 0.98 and 0.95. Simulate 1,000 days (14.2% were high-vol).
- filtered classification accuracy: **94.2%**
- always guessing "low vol": 85.8%

**Code:** `t52_regimes` (`hamilton_filter`)
**Watch out:** accuracy must be judged against the trivial baseline, and only filtered probabilities are usable live.

## 53. Kalman filter and state-space models

**Equations** (time-varying hedge ratio)
```
measurement:  y_t = beta_t * x_t + e_t        var(e) = R
state:        beta_t = beta_{t-1} + w_t       var(w) = Q
predict: P = P + Q
update:  K = P x / (x^2 P + R);  beta = beta + K (y - x beta);  P = (1 - K x) P
```

**Worked example.** True beta drifts from 1.0 to 1.5 over 600 days. RMSE of the beta estimate (day 100 onward):

| Estimator | RMSE |
|---|---|
| Rolling OLS, 60-day window | 0.0536 |
| Kalman, Q = 1e-6 | 0.1240 (too sluggish) |
| Kalman, Q = 1e-5 | 0.0865 |
| Kalman, **Q = 1e-4** | **0.0502** |
| Kalman, Q = 1e-3 | 0.0907 (too noisy) |

**Code:** `t53_kalman`
**Watch out:** Q is the tuning knob. Too small lags the truth, too large chases noise.

## 54. Extreme value theory

**Formulas** (peaks over threshold, generalized Pareto)
```
excesses y = loss - u for losses above threshold u;  m = mean(y), s2 = var(y)
xi   = 0.5 * (1 - m^2 / s2)            (method of moments)
beta = 0.5 * m * (m^2 / s2 + 1)
VaR_p = u + (beta/xi) * ( (n/N_u * (1 - p))^(-xi) - 1 )
```

**Worked example.** 20,000 daily losses from t(4) at 1% std (true tail index xi = 0.25). Threshold u = 1.49% (95th percentile).
- estimated xi = **0.223**, beta = 0.00608
- 99.9% VaR: **GPD 5.29%**, empirical 5.03%, "true" (4-million-point sample) 5.08%, **normal 3.15%**

The normal model understates the 99.9% loss by about 40%.

**Code:** `t54_evt`
**Watch out:** the threshold choice matters. Check that results are stable across nearby thresholds.

## 55. Copulas and tail dependence

**Formulas**
```
Sklar:  F(x1, x2) = C( F1(x1), F2(x2) )
Clayton lower-tail dependence:  lambda_L = 2^(-1/theta)
Gaussian copula: no tail dependence for rho < 1.   Student-t copula: positive tail dependence.
```

**Worked example.** Two assets with correlation 0.5. Probability both fall below their own 1% quantile on the same day (600,000 simulated days):

| Model | Joint 1% exceedance |
|---|---|
| Independent | 0.0001 |
| Gaussian copula | 0.00128 |
| Student-t copula (4 df) | **0.00282** |

Same correlation, but crashes cluster more than twice as often under the t copula. Clayton with theta = 2 has lower-tail dependence 0.707.

**Code:** `t55_copulas`
**Watch out:** correlation alone does not describe joint extremes.

## 56. Survival analysis and hazard models

**Formula** (Kaplan-Meier)
```
S(t) = product over event times t_i <= t of ( 1 - d_i / n_i )
d_i = events at t_i, n_i = number still at risk just before t_i (censored items count until censoring)
```

**Worked example.** Trade durations in days (1 = closed by stop or target, 0 = still open when the data ended): `3(1), 5(1), 5(0), 8(1), 10(0), 12(1), 15(1), 20(0)`.

| Time | At risk | Events | S(t) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 8 | 1 | 0.875 |
| 5 | 7 | 1 | 0.750 |
| 8 | 5 | 1 | 0.600 |
| 12 | 3 | 1 | 0.400 |
| 15 | 2 | 1 | 0.200 |

After 8 days, 60% of trades are still open. Dropping the open trades instead of treating them as censored would bias the durations short.

**Code:** `t56_survival` (`kaplan_meier`)
